# Testing a term

## The entry test

A word gets an entry only if the project uses it differently from its everyday meaning, two words compete for it, or it has already caused confusion. Ask the three questions in turn; one yes is enough, three noes mean no entry.

A frequent miss is a word that looks ordinary and is not: if two people in the project would answer "what is a X?" differently, the second question already says yes.

## Separating a word with two meanings

1. Collect two or three real sentences where the word appears (from the code, notes or the request).
2. For each sentence, say what the word stands for there.
3. If the answers differ, the word is carrying two concepts. Give each concept its own term. Keep the existing word for the meaning most of the project uses and choose a new word for the other; the old use of the word for the second meaning goes under that term's Don't call it.
4. Check that each new term passes the entry test on its own.

## The nearest-neighbour case

For a new term, name the concept it is most easily mistaken for and make up one concrete case that belongs to one and not the other. Write that case as the entry's Distinct from line or its Example.

- If no such case exists, the two are one concept: merge them.
- If the only difference is where the data is stored, it is not a domain difference: do not create two terms.
- A case that needs a long explanation means the definition is not yet sharp; rewrite the meaning before writing the entry.

## Competing words

When two words are in use for one concept, pick the one the owner and the domain's users say aloud. The other goes under Don't call it. Do not keep both "for now": the drift is the cost the entry exists to remove.
